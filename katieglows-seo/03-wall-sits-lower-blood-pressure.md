# Do Wall Sits Lower Blood Pressure? Here's What the Research Actually Says

Yes, and by more than most people expect. In 2023, researchers published one of the largest reviews ever done on exercise and blood pressure, and the exercise that came out on top wasn't running, cycling or lifting weights. It was the humble isometric hold: planks and, above all, **wall sits**.

Here's what they found, what it means for you, and how to do the routine they studied.

## The study, in plain English

The paper appeared in the *British Journal of Sports Medicine* (Edwards and colleagues, 2023). The team pooled 270 randomised controlled trials, published between 1990 and early 2023, covering 15,827 people. They compared five types of exercise:

- Aerobic exercise (walking, running, cycling)
- Dynamic resistance training (weights)
- Combined aerobic and resistance training
- High-intensity interval training (HIIT)
- Isometric exercise (holding a static position, such as a wall sit or plank)

All five lowered blood pressure. Isometric exercise lowered it the most.

## How much did blood pressure drop?

Average reductions in resting blood pressure, by exercise type:

| Exercise type | Systolic drop (top number) | Diastolic drop (bottom number) |
|---------------|---------------------------|-------------------------------|
| **Isometric (wall sits, planks)** | **8.2 mmHg** | **4.0 mmHg** |
| Combined aerobic + weights | 6.0 mmHg | 2.5 mmHg |
| Dynamic resistance (weights) | 4.6 mmHg | 3.0 mmHg |
| Aerobic (running, cycling) | 4.5 mmHg | 2.5 mmHg |
| HIIT | 4.1 mmHg | 2.5 mmHg |

For context, an 8 mmHg drop in systolic pressure is in the range doctors hope to see from a first blood pressure medication. The wall sit specifically was highlighted as the most effective single exercise within the isometric group.

## Why holding still lowers blood pressure

It sounds backwards. During an isometric hold, the working muscles squeeze the blood vessels running through them, which briefly *raises* pressure. When you release, blood rushes back through, and the vessel lining releases compounds that relax and widen the arteries. Repeat that a few times a week and the vessels appear to adapt, staying more relaxed at rest.

That's the leading explanation. The researchers were clear that more work is needed on the mechanism, but the effect itself showed up consistently across trials.

## The routine that was actually tested

Most of the isometric trials used a very simple protocol, and it's worth copying rather than inventing your own:

- **4 holds of 2 minutes each**
- **2 minutes of rest between holds**
- **3 times a week**
- Roughly 8 weeks before measuring the change

Total time: about 14 minutes, three days a week.

Two minutes is a long wall sit if you're new to them. The trials sometimes used lower-intensity holds (a shallower sit) to make two minutes achievable. So: **start with a shallower position and hit the two minutes, rather than going to parallel and stopping at 30 seconds.** Depth can increase as you get stronger. ## Who should be careful

Isometric exercise causes a temporary rise in blood pressure *during* the hold, especially if you hold your breath. That's the main reason for caution:

- If your blood pressure is high and not yet controlled, or you have a heart condition, talk to your doctor before starting.
- **Never hold your breath.** Breathe slowly and steadily the whole time.
- Stop if you feel dizzy, light-headed, or get chest discomfort.
- If you're on blood pressure medication, keep taking it. This is something to add, and your doctor can adjust the medication if your readings improve.

## How to measure whether it's working

1. Buy a validated upper-arm home monitor (they're inexpensive) and take readings the same way each time: seated, feet flat, after five quiet minutes, morning and evening.
2. Record a week of readings *before* you start, so you have a real baseline.
3. Do the 4 × 2-minute routine three times a week for eight weeks.
4. Record another week of readings. Compare the averages, not single readings.

Bring both sets to your GP. Numbers you've measured yourself over weeks are far more useful than a single reading at the surgery.

## Frequently asked questions

**How quickly do wall sits lower blood pressure?**
The trials measured after roughly eight weeks. Some people notice changes sooner, but give it two months before judging.

**Do I have to do the full two minutes?**
The tested protocol used two-minute holds, but at a manageable intensity. A shallower sit for two minutes is closer to the study than a deep sit for 30 seconds.

**Are planks as good as wall sits?**
Both are isometric and both worked in the review. Wall sits came out slightly ahead and are easier for most people over 50, because there's no wrist or shoulder load and nothing on the floor.

**Can wall sits replace my blood pressure medication?**
No. They can lower your readings, and your doctor may adjust your prescription as a result, but that's a decision for your doctor with your numbers in front of them.
