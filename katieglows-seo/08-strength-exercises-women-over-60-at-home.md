# 6 Strength Exercises for Women Over 60 You Can Do at Home (No Equipment, Nothing on the Floor)

After 60, strength is the thing that decides what you can do: carry the shopping, get up from a low sofa, lift a grandchild, catch yourself when you trip. Muscle is lost steadily from midlife on, faster after menopause, and the only thing that reliably reverses it is asking the muscles to work.

The good news is that "asking them to work" doesn't need a gym, weights or getting down on the floor. These six moves use a wall, a chair and your own body weight. Twenty minutes, two or three times a week.

## Before you start

- Wear shoes with grip, or go barefoot on a non-slip floor.
- Use a sturdy chair without wheels, ideally with its back against a wall.
- Warm up for two minutes: march on the spot, roll the shoulders, swing the arms.
- Work at an effort where the last two or three repetitions feel hard but your form stays clean. If they feel easy, slow the movement down; if you can't finish with good form, do fewer.
- Breathe out on the hard part. Never hold your breath.

## 1. Sit-to-stand

The most useful exercise on this list. It's a squat with a safety net.

1. Sit toward the front of the chair, feet flat and hip-width apart, arms crossed over your chest.
2. Lean forward slightly, push through your heels and stand up fully.
3. Lower yourself back down slowly, taking a full three seconds. The lowering is where the strength is built.

**Do:** 8–12 repetitions, 2 sets.
**Easier:** use your hands on your thighs to push. **Harder:** hold a bag of flour or a filled water bottle against your chest, or pause for two seconds just above the seat.

## 2. Wall sit

A static hold that builds thigh endurance and, as a 2023 review found, lowers blood pressure more than any other exercise type.

1. Back flat against the wall, feet out in front, slide down until thighs are at or a little above parallel.
2. Knees over ankles, weight in the heels, breathe steadily.
3. Hold, then slide back up.

**Do:** 20–60 seconds, 2 holds. Build slowly; the full guide is here: [Wall sits for women over 50](/wall-sits-for-women-over-50).

## 3. Wall push-up

All the benefit of a push-up for the chest, shoulders and arms, with none of the floor.

1. Stand facing a wall, arm's length away, hands flat on the wall at shoulder height and slightly wider than your shoulders.
2. Keeping your body in one straight line (don't let the hips sag or stick out), bend the elbows and bring your chest toward the wall.
3. Push back to the start.

**Do:** 10–15 repetitions, 2 sets.
**Harder:** step your feet further from the wall, or use a sturdy kitchen counter instead of the wall.

## 4. Standing calf raise

Calves matter for walking, balance and circulation, and they're neglected.

1. Stand behind the chair, fingertips resting on the back for balance.
2. Rise up onto the balls of your feet as high as you can, pause for a second at the top.
3. Lower slowly over three seconds.

**Do:** 12–15 repetitions, 2 sets.
**Harder:** one leg at a time.

## 5. Chair-supported hip hinge (the "good morning")

Trains the back of the body: hamstrings, glutes and lower back, the muscles that keep you upright.

1. Stand behind the chair, hands lightly on the back, feet hip-width apart, soft knees.
2. Push your hips backward as if closing a drawer with your bottom, letting your chest come forward. Keep your back flat; the movement is at the hips, not the waist.
3. Go until you feel a stretch in the backs of the thighs, then squeeze the glutes and stand tall.

**Do:** 10–12 repetitions, 2 sets.

## 6. Standing row with a towel

Upper back and arms, which hold the shoulders back and stop the forward hunch.

1. Loop a bath towel around a sturdy door handle or banister post, hold one end in each hand, and step back until there's tension.
2. Stand tall, then pull your elbows back past your body, squeezing the shoulder blades together.
3. Release slowly.

**Do:** 12–15 repetitions, 2 sets.
**Harder:** lean back further to increase the load. (Check the handle first.)

## The 20-minute plan

| | Monday | Wednesday | Friday |
|---|---|---|---|
| Warm-up | 2 min march | 2 min march | 2 min march |
| 1. Sit-to-stand | 2 × 10 | 2 × 10 | 2 × 12 |
| 2. Wall sit | 2 × 30 s | 2 × 30 s | 2 × 40 s |
| 3. Wall push-up | 2 × 12 | 2 × 12 | 2 × 15 |
| 4. Calf raise | 2 × 15 | 2 × 15 | 2 × 15 |
| 5. Hip hinge | 2 × 10 | 2 × 10 | 2 × 12 |
| 6. Towel row | 2 × 12 | 2 × 12 | 2 × 15 |

Rest about 45 seconds between sets. Every two weeks, add a repetition or two, or a few seconds to the wall sit. That slow increase is the whole method; it's called progressive overload and it is the only thing that keeps producing results.

## Make the strength stick

- **Protein.** Muscle is built from protein and most women over 50 eat too little. Aim for 25–30 g per meal ([why, and how](/protein-for-women-over-50)).
- **Rest days.** Muscle grows between sessions, not during them. Three sessions a week with a day between is plenty.
- **Walking on the other days.** Walking keeps you moving without competing with recovery.
- **Balance work.** Strength and balance protect each other; add [these balance exercises](/balance-exercises-over-60) on walking days.

## Frequently asked questions

**Is it safe to start strength training at 65 or 70?**
Yes, and it's one of the best things you can do at any age. Start with the easier versions, progress slowly, and check with your GP first if you have a heart condition, osteoporosis or a joint replacement.

**Will I get bulky?**
No. Women over 60 build muscle slowly and modestly. What you'll get is firmer, stronger and more upright.

**How soon will I feel a difference?**
Most people notice stairs and getting out of chairs feeling easier within three to four weeks. Visible changes take a couple of months.

**Do I need weights eventually?**
Not necessarily. Household objects (a bag of flour, a full water bottle, a rucksack with books) add load when you need it. A pair of light dumbbells is a nice upgrade, not a requirement.
