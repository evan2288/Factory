# Balance After 60: Take the 10-Second Test, Then Do These 8 Exercises

Here's a test that takes ten seconds and tells you something important. Stand on one leg, hands by your sides, and count. If you can get to ten, good. If you can't, it's worth knowing, because balance is trainable, and it's one of the few things that reliably protects you from the single biggest health risk after 60: a fall.

## The 10-second test

A 2022 study followed more than 1,700 adults aged 51 to 75 for seven years. Those who could not hold a single-leg stand for 10 seconds had a markedly higher risk of dying in that period than those who could. That's not because standing on one leg is magic; it's because balance is a good summary of leg strength, coordination, joint health and the nervous system all at once.

**How to do it:**
1. Stand near a counter or wall (don't hold on, but have it there).
2. Lift one foot a few centimetres off the floor, keep the other leg straight-ish, hands relaxed by your sides, eyes on a fixed spot ahead.
3. Count to ten. Stop if you touch the counter, put the foot down, or have to swing your arms wildly.
4. Do the other leg.

**Benchmarks:** most healthy adults in their 60s can manage 10–20 seconds with a little practice; into the 70s, 10 seconds is a solid target. Under 5 seconds means balance should be a priority this month, not a someday thing.

## Why balance goes, and why it comes back

Balance relies on three systems: the inner ear, the eyes, and the sense of where your joints are in space (proprioception), all feeding into leg and hip muscles that make the corrections. Every one of these dulls with age and with inactivity. Every one of them also improves with practice, at any age. Trials of balance training in older adults cut falls by roughly 20–25%, and structured programmes like tai chi by as much as 50%.

## The 8 exercises

Do these at a kitchen counter or behind a sturdy chair, so there's something to grab. Bare feet or grippy shoes. Ten to fifteen minutes, three times a week is the evidence-based dose; a couple of minutes daily works well too.

**1. Sit-to-stand (slow).** From a chair, stand up without using your hands, then lower over a full three seconds. 10 repetitions. The most functional fall-prevention exercise there is.

**2. Heel raises.** Hands on the counter, rise onto the balls of the feet, lower slowly. 15 repetitions. Strengthens the calves, which make many of the small balance corrections.

**3. Single-leg stand.** The test, as an exercise. Hold 10–30 seconds each side, fingertips hovering above the counter. When 30 seconds is easy, try it with your eyes closed (fingertips *on* the counter for that one).

**4. Heel-to-toe walk.** Walk a straight line along the kitchen floor placing the heel of one foot directly in front of the toes of the other, as if on a tightrope. Ten steps forward, then turn and come back. Hand trailing along the counter if needed.

**5. Side steps.** Hands on the counter, step sideways along it, ten steps each way. Then repeat without hands.

**6. Wall sit.** Back to the wall, slide down, hold 20–60 seconds. Builds the thigh endurance that stops legs giving out on stairs; full guide here: [Wall sits for women over 50](/wall-sits-for-women-over-50).

**7. Clock reach.** Stand on one leg beside the counter. Imagine a clock face on the floor and reach the free foot lightly toward 12, 3 and 6 o'clock, returning to standing each time. Five circuits each leg.

**8. Tai chi-style weight shift.** Feet wide, knees soft. Slowly shift all your weight to the left leg, pause, then to the right, over about four seconds each way. Two minutes. Add a slow arm sweep once it feels natural.

## A simple weekly plan

- **Mon / Wed / Fri:** the eight exercises, 12–15 minutes, alongside or after your [strength session](/strength-exercises-women-over-60-at-home).
- **Every day:** the single-leg stand while the kettle boils. Thirty seconds each leg.
- **Every two weeks:** redo the 10-second test and write down your best time.

## Make the house safer too

Training balance is half the job; the other half is removing the things you'd trip over. Loose rugs, trailing cables, poor lighting on stairs, no grab rail in the shower. And shoes: slippers with no backs are involved in a lot of falls.

## Frequently asked questions

**Is it normal to wobble a lot at first?**
Yes. Wobbling is the nervous system practising. It gets quieter within a couple of weeks.

**I've had a fall already. Should I still do these?**
Ask your GP or physiotherapist first; they may want to check what caused the fall and adapt the exercises. Balance training after a fall is common and helpful, but with guidance.

**Does walking count as balance training?**
It helps, but it doesn't challenge balance enough on its own. Add the exercises above.

**How long before my balance improves?**
Most people notice within three to four weeks of regular practice. The 10-second test is the easiest way to see it.
